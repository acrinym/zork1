# Human Companion, Bond, and Optional Love Interest

## Status

Future-design material for the **Highly Extended Zork** lane.

This document does not implement a companion, create beads, alter Release `1230`, or claim product progress. It explores a major future direction that classic Zork never attempted: a fully physical human adventuring companion who may become the Adventurer's closest friend or love interest through actual shared play.

The relationship is **not a dating simulator**, dialogue-tree courtship layer, affection-meter minigame, or reward attached to collecting gifts.

The companion must be integrated deeply enough that her presence changes exploration, danger, evidence, custody, planning, humor, failure, recovery, and the meaning of returning home.

## Core proposition

Add one deeply authored human companion who is another causal adventurer in the world.

She has:

- her own body, location, posture, injuries, fatigue, warmth, light, equipment, and carrying limits;
- her own inventory and exact object custody;
- her own perceptions, incomplete knowledge, memories, interpretations, suspicions, and mistakes;
- her own goal, schedule, fears, boundaries, promises, and reasons to continue or leave;
- competence in some areas and real limitations in others;
- the ability to act, refuse, warn, rescue, retreat, disagree, investigate, conceal, forgive, resent, trust, love, or walk away;
- a history with the player built from specific events rather than an exposed relationship score.

She is not a follower object with dialogue. She is a second player-sized causal entity whose decisions remain authored and legible.

## Why this would transform Zork

Classic Zork is built around radical solitude.

The Adventurer enters a world of rooms, mechanisms, monsters, treasure, danger, and sarcastic narration with no enduring human equal beside him. Actors such as the thief, troll, cyclops, boatman, and other inhabitants are obstacles, mysteries, or bounded encounters rather than sustained companions.

A human companion changes the emotional geometry of the entire game:

- a warning may now be spoken by someone who remembers whether the player listened;
- a dangerous route becomes a coordination problem rather than only an inventory problem;
- an object may belong to her, be entrusted to the player, be lost by either person, or become evidence of a broken promise;
- a near-death has a witness and aftermath;
- a choice to retreat can express wisdom, fear, loyalty, cowardice, or care depending on the circumstances;
- returning to the House can mean returning **with someone**;
- an expedition history can preserve not only what happened, but what each person believed happened;
- a victory may feel hollow if the relationship was destroyed along the way;
- an ordinary meal, watch, repair, or shared silence can matter because the world around it has been dangerous.

This does not make Zork less puzzle-driven. It gives physical puzzles, consequences, and discoveries a second human perspective.

## Why the companion should be human

A magical familiar, talking animal, construct, spirit, or grue ally could be entertaining, but a human companion creates the strongest version of this idea.

She is vulnerable to the same darkness, cold, current, smoke, falling stone, betrayal, hunger, exhaustion, and bad decisions as the player.

She can:

- hold the other end of a rope;
- carry real weight;
- misunderstand a warning;
- become frightened without becoming useless;
- know something the player does not;
- need help without existing only to be rescued;
- judge what the player does to other people and creatures;
- imagine a future beyond the current puzzle;
- choose whether companionship remains practical, becomes friendship, becomes love, or ends.

Her humanity makes coordination, trust, injury, intimacy, and loss materially meaningful.

## One authored companion, not a roster

The strongest design is **one fully authored companion**, not a menu of interchangeable party members.

A roster would encourage broad archetypes, repeated recruitment structures, shallow banter, inventory-role optimization, and romance-option selection. That would move the game toward a party RPG and away from Zork's dense authored causality.

One person allows:

- exact memory of hundreds of consequential events;
- strong continuity across the House, Dam, forest, underground, museum, cuisine, archive, and later regions;
- specific humor rather than generic personality tags;
- meaningful absence;
- a relationship capable of deep friendship even when romance never occurs;
- replay differences based on one coherent person responding to different histories.

Temporary NPC cooperation may still exist, but the central companion remains singular.

## Working character candidate: Mara Tallow

The name and details are not locked, but a concrete candidate helps test the design.

### Identity

**Mara Tallow** is an adult human field surveyor, route historian, and practical expeditionist from a declining settlement at the edge of the Great Underground Empire.

She is not royalty, a chosen one, a princess, a healer assigned to support the hero, or a secret magical superbeing.

She is a professional whose work matters independently of the Adventurer.

### Temperament

Mara is:

- observant;
- dryly funny rather than constantly quippy;
- skeptical of treasure hunters and official imperial maps;
- patient with mechanisms and evidence;
- impatient with bluffing, waste, and avoidable recklessness;
- brave enough to continue while still capable of fear;
- slow to trust declarations and quick to notice behavior;
- capable of tenderness without becoming emotionally available on demand.

She does not exist to admire the player. Respect must be earned in both directions.

### Skills

Mara is especially capable with:

- maps, route memory, measurements, and contradictory geography;
- rope, anchors, knots, ladders, crossings, and field safety;
- reading structural danger in dams, bridges, tunnels, and ruins;
- preserving notes, sketches, samples, and provenance;
- patient observation of tracks, water, weather, machinery, and damaged surfaces;
- preparing a route before crossing it;
- noticing when an official explanation does not match physical evidence.

She is not automatically good at:

- sword fighting;
- magical rituals;
- every historical language;
- identifying every creature or substance;
- solving canonical puzzles;
- trusting improvised plans merely because the player proposes them.

Her competence opens alternate approaches without making her a walking answer key.

### Independent goal

Mara is attempting to reconstruct the **Last Honest Survey of the Great Underground Empire**.

Official route records were altered after an old disaster, political embarrassment, or failed imperial project. Some routes were erased, some were declared impossible, and some surviving maps contradict the physical world.

She wants to determine:

- what actually happened;
- who changed the record;
- which missing people or expeditions were blamed falsely;
- whether the altered geography concealed danger, treasure, crime, or something stranger;
- whether the Empire can still be mapped truthfully at all.

This goal naturally intersects with the House archive, contradictory Dungeon/Zork geography, museum evidence, damaged warnings, expedition records, and regional expansion without making her subordinate to any one system.

She may cooperate with the player because their paths overlap. She continues because her work is hers.

## First meeting: Flood Control Dam #3

The first meeting should establish equality through action.

Mara should not be standing safely in town waiting to join, nor helplessly imprisoned for the player to rescue.

A strong first encounter places the two adventurers on opposite sides of a developing Dam emergency.

Possible state:

- a maintenance platform has shifted;
- water is rising or current is changing;
- a gate mechanism is partially engaged;
- the player has access to one control, tool, or route;
- Mara has the rope, measurement, warning knowledge, or second control required to make the situation survivable;
- neither can complete the safe solution alone;
- both can choose a selfish escape that increases the other's danger;
- both can witness whether the other keeps a promise under pressure.

The player may first hear another human voice through machinery and water before seeing her.

The meeting demonstrates:

- natural-address parser commands;
- two-person physical coordination;
- incomplete information on both sides;
- independent judgment;
- immediate consequences for trust;
- competence as the first source of attraction, if attraction ever develops.

The first relationship event is not flirtation. It is **competence witnessed under danger**.

## Where she exists in the world

Mara must not be permanently glued to the player's location.

Her presence should alternate with meaningful absence.

### Field camp

Before trust is established, she maintains her own camp, cache, route notes, supplies, and schedule.

The player may:

- visit;
- find her absent;
- leave a note or object;
- discover that she moved camp because of danger;
- be refused entry to a private container;
- notice evidence of an expedition she did not discuss;
- receive a message asking for help or warning him away.

### The House

The House may eventually become a shared expedition base, but only through authored consent and history.

Possible developments include:

- a guest room, bed, cot, or deliberately claimed corner;
- a separate locked or private chest;
- a desk with her map sheets and field journal;
- her boots, rope, coat, or tools appearing where she actually left them;
- correspondence addressed to her rather than the player;
- disagreements over storage, risk, damaged objects, visitors, or dangerous materials;
- shared repairs and preparations;
- the possibility that she leaves the House after a breach of trust;
- the quiet significance of her returning.

She does not become House property, staff, or a permanent reward.

### Expeditions

Some regions are traveled together. Others are intentionally solo.

Reasons for separation may include:

- two routes must be investigated simultaneously;
- she pursues her own lead;
- one person must operate machinery while the other travels;
- a region is too dangerous for both to risk;
- injury or exhaustion requires one person to remain behind;
- an argument causes temporary separation;
- the world physically divides them;
- one person chooses not to participate in a morally unacceptable action.

Solitude remains part of Zork. The companion makes solitude more noticeable rather than eliminating it.

## The companion as a second causal adventurer

### Physical state

Mara has real, persistent state:

- location;
- facing or attention where relevant;
- posture and movement;
- health and specific injuries;
- fatigue, warmth, wetness, smoke exposure, poison, contamination, and other authored conditions;
- carried weight and mobility;
- light source and remaining fuel;
- weapon or tool readiness;
- exact inventory and nested containers;
- clothing and protection;
- whether she can see, hear, reach, hold, climb, swim, or assist.

The game must not teleport her, duplicate her equipment, or reset injuries merely to simplify a scene.

### Perception and belief

Mara knows only what she:

- witnessed;
- was told;
- inferred;
- measured;
- read;
- remembered;
- discovered through another source.

She may be wrong.

She may distinguish:

- witnessed fact;
- player claim;
- trusted testimony;
- rumor;
- working theory;
- contradiction;
- deliberate lie;
- unresolved uncertainty.

If the player lies, she does not receive omniscient proof. She notices inconsistencies only when evidence appears.

### Goals and priorities

Her immediate decision-making should be driven by authored priorities such as:

1. survive an active danger;
2. prevent another person's immediate death when reasonably possible;
3. protect irreplaceable evidence tied to her own mission;
4. keep or break specific promises;
5. pursue the current shared plan;
6. preserve her own autonomy and boundaries;
7. continue her independent survey work.

These priorities may conflict. The interesting moments occur when they do.

### Promises

Promises should be explicit world state rather than vague flavor.

Examples:

- “Wait here until I return.”
- “Do not open that gate.”
- “I will bring your notebook back.”
- “We leave if the light falls below half.”
- “I will not give the specimen to the curator.”
- “I will tell you before I go below the Dam again.”

Breaking a promise matters because the exact promise, circumstances, and consequences remain known.

## Relationship architecture without an affection meter

The game may internally retain several qualitative dimensions, but it should never expose a single romance or approval number.

Useful dimensions include:

- **trust** — whether the player keeps promises, tells the truth, returns custody, and behaves predictably;
- **respect** — whether the player demonstrates competence, judgment, curiosity, restraint, and willingness to learn;
- **safety** — whether she expects the player to protect, endanger, coerce, abandon, or use her;
- **resentment** — specific unresolved injuries, betrayals, dismissals, thefts, or preventable losses;
- **affection** — fondness built through shared experience, humor, care, and chosen presence;
- **intimacy** — willingness to reveal fear, history, uncertainty, grief, hope, or desire;
- **commitment** — whether each person plans around a shared future rather than only the current expedition.

These dimensions are evidence-backed.

They change through events such as:

- returning for her when escape was possible;
- listening when she identifies a structural danger;
- admitting uncertainty instead of bluffing;
- trusting her with a unique object;
- returning something she entrusted to the player;
- sharing scarce light, food, warmth, or carrying burden;
- respecting a refusal;
- apologizing for a specific action and then behaving differently;
- protecting her work when she is absent;
- refusing to use her as bait;
- abandoning her;
- stealing from her;
- lying about an injury or danger;
- taking credit for her discovery;
- repeatedly treating her warnings as disposable dialogue;
- touching or kissing her after she has refused;
- deciding together to retreat.

The player cannot grind trust by repeating a polite command.

## Parser interaction

The companion should expand parser play rather than become a dialogue-menu overlay.

### Direct address

Examples:

```text
> MARA, HOLD THE ROPE
> MARA, WATCH THE EAST PASSAGE
> ASK MARA TO LOWER THE LANTERN
> TELL MARA ABOUT THE BLOOD-STAINED WARNING
> SHOW THE BROKEN GAUGE TO MARA
> GIVE MARA THE WAXED MAP
> ASK MARA WHO MOVED THE PACK
> MARA, TAKE THE BOAT WHILE I OPEN THE GATE
> FOLLOW MARA
> ASK MARA TO FOLLOW ME
> WAIT FOR MARA
> LEAVE A NOTE FOR MARA
```

### Social actions

Social verbs should be specific and context-aware:

```text
> THANK MARA
> APOLOGIZE TO MARA
> ADMIT THAT I LIED
> ASK MARA TO STAY
> TELL MARA I AM AFRAID
> PROMISE TO RETURN BEFORE DARK
> ASK MARA WHAT SHE THINKS
> COMFORT MARA
> EMBRACE MARA
> KISS MARA
```

A command does not force the emotional result.

`TRUST ME` cannot manufacture trust. `APOLOGIZE` cannot erase consequences. `KISS MARA` may be welcomed, refused, misunderstood, badly timed, or relationship-ending depending on history and context.

### Joint physical actions

Two-person actions are one of the strongest reasons to build the system:

```text
> PULL THE CHAIN WITH MARA
> HOLD THE LADDER FOR MARA
> ASK MARA TO BRACE THE DOOR
> LOWER MARA INTO THE SHAFT
> HAVE MARA LOWER ME INTO THE SHAFT
> CARRY THE INJURED SURVEYOR TOGETHER
> ROW WHILE MARA STEERS
> COVER MARA WHILE SHE CROSSES
> PASS THE ROPE AROUND THE PILLAR
> LIFT THE BEAM TOGETHER
```

Coordination should depend on position, readiness, trust, understanding, strength, tools, and danger—not only whether the syntax is recognized.

## Autonomy and refusal

Mara must be able to refuse.

Refusal may arise because:

- the action is suicidal;
- the player concealed relevant danger;
- it violates a promise or boundary;
- she believes the object belongs elsewhere;
- her own goal takes priority;
- she is injured, exhausted, frightened, or physically unable;
- the player has repeatedly treated her as disposable;
- she has a safer alternative;
- she believes the player is wrong.

A refusal should usually include a legible reason based on known state.

She may also act without being ordered:

- catch a falling object;
- shout a warning;
- retreat from fire;
- pull the player from current;
- protect her notebook;
- close a dangerous route;
- refuse to wait inside an obviously collapsing room;
- leave a note and pursue a lead;
- interrupt an action that would knowingly kill someone;
- preserve a sample before the player cooks it;
- move a wet rope to dry;
- wake the player when danger changes.

Autonomy must be bounded and authored. She should not randomly manipulate puzzles or possessions for artificial drama.

## She is not a hint dispenser

Mara must not solve the game for the player.

Her observations may:

- describe something she can physically see;
- recall evidence she actually encountered;
- question an inconsistency;
- suggest a category of action rather than the exact solution;
- admit that she has no idea;
- make a plausible but wrong inference;
- ask the player to explain his reasoning;
- provide expertise in her established fields.

She should never reveal:

- unseen object locations;
- hidden commands;
- puzzle solutions outside her knowledge;
- omniscient actor state;
- future consequences without evidence.

The player may ask repeatedly, but repetition does not generate new knowledge.

## Love without a dating simulator

Romance is a possible development of the adventure bond, not a separate game loop.

### No romance economy

There is no:

- visible affection bar;
- gift-spam optimization;
- flirt option attached to every conversation;
- list of approval gains and losses;
- mandatory date sequence;
- romance-exclusive combat bonus;
- “complete her quest to unlock her” structure;
- assumption that kindness entitles the player to love.

### Romance grows from adventure history

Potentially intimate moments emerge because of what happened:

- one person stays awake while the other sleeps after a near-death;
- a hand remains clasped after a dangerous crossing is over;
- they share a meal made from scarce expedition supplies;
- one repairs the other's damaged gear without being asked;
- an argument finally reveals what each person was actually afraid of;
- one admits that returning alone is no longer an acceptable future;
- they find an old expedition record describing another pair who failed each other;
- one person returns after having every practical reason not to;
- they laugh at a catastrophic failed dish after surviving something worse;
- a quiet room in the House begins to contain the ordinary evidence of two lives.

The emotional scene is earned by the causal history around it.

### Consent and interpretation

The player may express interest through words or physical actions, but Mara's response depends on:

- prior trust and affection;
- current danger;
- privacy;
- whether the action respects earlier boundaries;
- whether she believes the player is sincere;
- whether unresolved betrayal makes intimacy impossible;
- her own desire.

A refusal remains a refusal.

Repeated pressure is remembered as pressure, not courtship.

### Deep friendship remains complete

The companion system must support a profound non-romantic bond with equal narrative weight.

A player who never pursues romance should not receive a hollow or incomplete version of Mara.

Possible enduring outcomes include:

- trusted expedition partners;
- chosen family;
- close friendship with separate futures;
- professional respect after personal conflict;
- reconciliation without romance;
- separation with affection intact;
- love acknowledged but not acted upon;
- mutual commitment.

Romance is one truthful outcome, not the completion state of the relationship.

## Relationship stages as authored history

The relationship should not be presented as rigid levels, but several broad phases help design scenes.

### 1. Wary coexistence

They cooperate because circumstances require it.

Mara watches custody, promises, recklessness, and competence closely.

### 2. Functional alliance

They begin making plans that assume the other's participation.

Equipment, route decisions, and evidence are shared cautiously.

### 3. Trusted companionship

Each person voluntarily accepts risk for the other's goals.

The House, field camp, and expedition preparation begin to reflect two people.

### 4. Emotional intimacy

Fear, grief, hope, shame, or desire is discussed without immediate practical necessity.

This phase may remain deeply platonic.

### 5. Chosen partnership

They make decisions based on a shared future.

For a romantic path, love may finally be spoken or physically acknowledged here. The actual commitment is already visible in behavior.

### 6. Fracture, repair, or separation

Any phase may be damaged.

Repair requires:

- acknowledgment of the exact harm;
- credible change;
- time;
- sometimes restitution;
- the injured person's willingness.

Not every relationship can or should be repaired.

## Conflict must be real

A companion who always agrees becomes decorative.

Mara may disagree about:

- killing or sparing the troll;
- using a live creature as bait;
- consuming a unique specimen;
- opening a dangerous route;
- risking the House to contain a cursed object;
- giving evidence to the museum;
- cooperating with the thief;
- entering Hades;
- continuing after supplies or light fall below an agreed threshold;
- whether an imperial record should be preserved, exposed, or destroyed;
- whether the player's curiosity has become recklessness;
- whether her own mission is consuming her judgment.

The player may be right. Mara may be right. Both may be partly wrong.

The world should decide through consequence and evidence rather than a morality tooltip.

## Danger, rescue, and loss

### She can need help without becoming a damsel

Mara may be trapped, injured, poisoned, lost, captured, or overwhelmed because the world is dangerous.

The same must be true of the player.

Rescue should be reciprocal across the full game:

- she pulls the player from current;
- the player returns for her after a collapse;
- she holds off an actor while the player repairs a route;
- the player carries her after an injury;
- she refuses evacuation until a third person is safe;
- either person may make the wrong call.

### She is not an escort mission

The player should not constantly babysit her health or pathfinding.

Mara:

- avoids obvious hazards;
- communicates when she cannot follow;
- protects herself where reasonable;
- uses known routes;
- refuses impossible commands;
- does not repeatedly step into the same trap;
- can remain safely behind when appropriate.

Her vulnerability creates stakes, not maintenance chores.

### Death

Mara may be able to die, but her death must never be cheap drama.

It should require a real causal chain and should not exist merely to motivate the player.

Possible rules:

- many dangers produce injury, separation, capture, or rescue windows before death;
- she uses her own knowledge and self-preservation;
- the player receives physical warnings where credible;
- preventable death records exact promises, warnings, choices, objects, and missed opportunities;
- her own autonomous mistake may contribute;
- she may sacrifice herself only when consistent with her priorities, not because the plot demands tragedy;
- the game never reduces her life to a score penalty.

If she dies, the world continues with:

- her actual possessions and custody;
- unfinished survey work;
- correspondence;
- witnesses;
- House evidence;
- museum records;
- the player's conduct history;
- the possibility that the player cannot emotionally or practically replace her.

## Integration with existing Highly Extended Zork directions

### Causal death and warning architecture

Mara can:

- issue warnings;
- hear the player dismiss them;
- misunderstand warnings herself;
- become part of a rescue chain;
- testify about a near-death;
- remember whether the player knew the risk;
- alter narrator culpability through witnessed behavior.

A death involving two adventurers can distinguish:

- ignorance;
- disagreement;
- coercion;
- sacrifice;
- betrayal;
- failed rescue;
- mutual mistake;
- deliberate risk accepted by both.

### Physical expedition stash and Zork Plus

The House stash becomes more interesting with two owners.

It may contain:

- player property;
- Mara's property;
- shared expedition equipment;
- objects entrusted temporarily;
- supplies one person packed without the other's knowledge;
- a promised reserve that must not be consumed;
- evidence of who prepared the expedition.

Veteran Expedition loadout planning becomes a real conversation and negotiation.

### Museum

Mara may:

- register survey evidence;
- dispute a curator's plaque;
- provide a map, rubbing, sample, or field measurement;
- refuse to surrender evidence tied to her mission;
- co-author an exhibit;
- discover that the museum's official geography is false;
- become angry if the player donates her property;
- leave her name off a discovery intentionally or demand proper credit.

She is not automatically museum staff.

### Ecology and fishing

Two people can:

- observe from different banks;
- net or release a specimen together;
- compare tracks and water conditions;
- keep a creature alive during transport;
- disagree about capture versus observation;
- rescue each other from current;
- preserve both scientific and culinary evidence.

### Cuisine

Food becomes relational without becoming a dating mechanic.

Examples:

- sharing the last safe ration;
- preparing food for an injured partner;
- respecting a food she refuses;
- learning a regional dish from her;
- ruining a meal together;
- preserving her portion when she is delayed;
- discovering that she packed something because she remembered a prior expedition;
- arguing over cooking a unique specimen;
- returning to the House to find a meal left warm after a dangerous absence.

### Thief

The thief may exploit the relationship:

- steal an entrusted object;
- impersonate a message;
- threaten one person to manipulate the other;
- return property selectively;
- provoke jealousy or mistrust through planted evidence;
- underestimate Mara;
- discover that two coordinated adversaries are harder to isolate.

The game must distinguish manipulation from arbitrary relationship drama.

### Troll

Mara may have her own opinion of the troll, recognize his behavior, negotiate differently, or become involved in the stolen-sword history.

She must not automatically become a second combatant who trivializes the encounter.

### Grue ecology

A companion intensifies darkness:

- two people make more noise;
- two light sources change visibility and fuel planning;
- one may hear something the other does not;
- separating in darkness becomes terrifying;
- a call and answer can reveal position to both companion and colony;
- one person may hold light while the other acts;
- losing sight of Mara can matter more than losing a map.

### House and archive

The House can retain:

- separate journals;
- contradictory accounts;
- shared promises;
- objects each person filed;
- letters addressed separately;
- damage each person caused or repaired;
- evidence of absence and return;
- a completed expedition box that records two perspectives without pretending they were identical.

Mara's journal should never become a hidden omniscient transcript. It records only what she knew and chose to write.

## Replayability

The same companion can produce radically different histories.

Possible outcomes include:

- she never joins beyond the Dam alliance;
- she becomes a recurring independent ally;
- she moves into the House as an expedition partner;
- she becomes the player's closest friend;
- they fall in love;
- affection exists but timing or trust prevents partnership;
- a betrayal causes permanent separation;
- they reconcile after restitution;
- she completes her survey and leaves;
- the player abandons her mission for treasure;
- she abandons the player to save her work or another person;
- one dies;
- both survive but choose different futures;
- they begin a Veteran Expedition together.

The canonical score does not measure these outcomes.

The expedition archive records concrete history:

- promises kept and broken;
- rescues;
- shared discoveries;
- custody disputes;
- separations;
- injuries;
- acts of trust;
- final relationship and unresolved questions.

## Tone

The relationship must still sound like Zork.

That means:

- dry wit;
- absurd physical consequences;
- strange bureaucracy;
- objects that matter too much;
- serious danger next to ridiculous failure;
- no endless modern therapy dialogue;
- no constant banter;
- no melodrama unsupported by events;
- emotional sincerity allowed to exist without the narrator immediately destroying it.

Mara may be funny because she observes the world sharply, not because every room triggers a joke.

Silence is important.

A single line after a dangerous event can carry more weight than a conversation wheel.

## What this system must never become

The companion must not become:

- a chatbot bolted onto the parser;
- an omniscient hint engine;
- an inventory mule;
- a healing station;
- a romance reward;
- a submissive follower;
- a constant banter generator;
- an escort objective;
- a generic approval meter;
- a source of random betrayal;
- an excuse to replace authored puzzles with AI improvisation;
- a second protagonist who steals all player agency;
- a person whose suffering exists only to deepen the hero;
- a mandatory partner in every room;
- a duplicate controller for canonical actors, score, or puzzles.

## Technical and design hazards

### Parser ambiguity

Commands involving two actors, two objects, pronouns, shared containers, and indirect orders can become confusing.

The design needs:

- reliable direct-address grammar;
- clear confirmation when an order is interpreted;
- explicit distinction between `GIVE`, `SHOW`, `ASK TO TAKE`, and `TAKE FROM`;
- stable pronoun and ownership handling;
- refusal messages that explain whether the problem is syntax, knowledge, willingness, or physical possibility.

### Branch explosion

Every major scene can multiply through presence, absence, trust, injury, knowledge, and relationship state.

Control this by:

- selecting a bounded set of truly companion-reactive events;
- allowing neutral fallback behavior where her presence is not materially relevant;
- deriving reactions from compact evidence fields;
- writing several strong state families rather than bespoke prose for every permutation;
- refusing to pretend that every object needs companion commentary.

### Companion pathfinding

She should travel through authored route logic, not a universal navigation simulator.

The game must know:

- whether she can physically traverse each relevant route;
- why she cannot follow;
- where she waits;
- how she rejoins;
- what happens if the route changes while separated.

### Save and restore

Native persistence must preserve:

- physical state;
- exact custody;
- knowledge and beliefs;
- promises;
- unresolved conflicts;
- injuries;
- current goals;
- relationship evidence;
- independent schedule;
- journal and correspondence state.

Restore must not repair the relationship or silently synchronize knowledge.

## Strongest first promoted product

If this direction is eventually selected, the first implementation should not attempt the full romance arc or every world integration at once.

It should build a complete **Companion Expedition Foundation** with:

1. one authored human companion;
2. the Dam mutual-rescue meeting;
3. direct-address parser grammar;
4. physical location, inventory, light, injury, and carrying state;
5. perception and evidence-limited knowledge;
6. one independent goal and route schedule;
7. promises, refusal, and bounded autonomy;
8. several two-person physical actions;
9. one separation and reunion sequence;
10. one real disagreement with multiple authored outcomes;
11. a field camp and one House integration path;
12. a deep friendship path complete in itself;
13. only the earliest possibility of romantic recognition, not a rushed relationship payoff;
14. native save, corruption testing, and exact restore;
15. no changes to original solo puzzle solvability unless explicitly authored as optional alternate routes.

That would be a complete player-facing product foundation, not a shallow demo.

## Final design judgment

This idea works because the companion is not an added feature attached to Zork.

She changes the meaning of the systems already imagined:

- danger has a witness;
- warnings become interpersonal;
- objects can be entrusted;
- routes can be coordinated;
- the House can be shared;
- food can express memory and care;
- archives can disagree;
- theft can manipulate trust;
- solitude can become loss;
- survival can become a promise kept to another person.

The romance, when it occurs, is not the system's purpose.
